# Quality checklist

## Mandatory points

- Horizontal 16:9 format.
- Clean white background.
- The character is present.
- The character carries the central action, not just a decorative role.
- No composition copied from an old example — a new metaphor was invented for the current text.
- The image is strange, creative, interesting.
- Sober and uncluttered, the main subject doesn't exceed about 60% of the image.
- A single central structure per image.
- Few annotations, short, legible.
- Orange is only used for the main path or arrows.
- Red is only used for key points, problems, warnings, or results.
- Blue is only used for complementary explanations, feedback, or system states.

## Failure signals

If any of these signals appear, regenerate or edit locally:

- A title like "Common pitfalls / Workflow / Architecture diagram / Roadmap" appears in the top-left
  corner.
- The character looks like a mascot, sticker, or cute cartoon.
- The image looks like a PPT, a course handout, a formal flowchart.
- Too many elements, too many arrows, too many nodes.
- The text turns into a long block of explanation.
- The background has a paper texture, a shadow, a gradient, a beige tint, noise.
- A real UI screenshot or high-tech interface.
- Significant mistakes in the annotations or illegible text.
- The image is too rigid, without an offbeat metaphor.
- Too close a resemblance to a composition already used in `assets/examples/`.

## Iteration method

- Too plain: make the character the subject of the action, add a strange but coherent metaphor.
- Too complex: remove nodes, keep only one action and 3 to 5 short annotations.
- Too cute: emphasize "deadpan," "neutral and serious expression," "not cute," "not a mascot."
- Too PPT: remove title, frame, regular grid, and excessive arrows, go back to a hand-drawn scene.
- Too close to an old example: keep the central idea, change the main object and the character's
  action.
- Incorrect text: prefer local editing; if there are too many mistakes, regenerate while reducing
  the number of annotations.

## Delivery criterion

A quality image must first give the reader a slight sense of offbeat strangeness, then let them grasp
the structure within a second.

If the first glance evokes a tutorial page rather than a strange product sketch on white paper, the
image doesn't meet the bar.
